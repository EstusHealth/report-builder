4 · Off-Road Assessment
Hearing & communication
First, because everything after it depends on instructions being understood. A cognitive score depressed by a missed instruction is not a cognitive finding.
▸ How to screen hearing and communication for the driving task
Hearing
  • You are not doing audiometry. You are establishing whether an instruction given from the passenger seat, over road noise, with the speaker's face turned away, will land.
  • Give a short instruction at conversational volume with your face turned away from them, from roughly the distance and angle the MDI will be at. Then repeat it with your face visible. A large difference between the two means they are lip-reading, which they will not be able to do while watching the road.
  • Check hearing aids are in, switched on and charged — this is one of the items on the set-up checklist for exactly this reason.
  • Note whether emergency sirens, indicator clicks or reversing sensors have been reported as inaudible.
Communication
  • Give a two-step then a three-step instruction and watch how it is executed — "turn left at the second street, then pull over past the crossing" is the form the on-road instruction actually takes.
  • Watch for whether they ask when they have not understood. A client who guesses rather than clarifies will guess at an intersection, and that is a safety finding rather than a language one.
  • Record processing delay: how long between the instruction and the action. On road that delay is metres.
  • Note interpreter need, English as an additional language, expressive difficulty (aphasia, dysarthria), and anything that changes how the MDI should phrase directions. That goes into the MDI briefing in Stage 3.
Visual screen
Three separate things are being screened here and they fail independently: acuity (can the detail be resolved at distance), fields (is anything missing from the periphery), and ocular motility (can the eyes be moved to where the information is). A client can read the bottom line of the chart and still be unsafe. Open the cards below for how to conduct each one.
▸ What the vision screen is testing — and what it does not test
The three screens
  • Visual acuity — the smallest high-contrast detail resolvable at distance. In driving terms: reading a sign or a number plate. It is the one measure with a hard licensing number attached to it, which is why it gets more attention than its predictive power deserves.
  • Visual fields — how much of the world is available without moving the eyes. In driving terms: the pedestrian at the kerb, the car in the adjacent lane, the cyclist coming into the intersection. Field loss is a far better predictor of crash risk than acuity, and the client is frequently unaware of it.
  • Ocular motility — whether the eyes can be moved accurately and comfortably to where the information is. In driving terms: the mirror check, the head-and-eye blind-spot check, and the scan across an intersection, which are sequences of large saccades under time pressure.
What this screen does not measure
  • Contrast sensitivity — dusk, rain, a grey car against wet road. Normal acuity with poor contrast sensitivity is common in cataract and in some neurological conditions, and it is a driving problem you will not see on a chart.
  • Glare recovery — headlights at night, low sun. Ask about it; you cannot test it here.
  • Useful field of view — the field a person can actually extract information from while doing something else. That is a divided-attention measure, and it is why the cognitive battery follows this screen rather than replacing it.
  • Eye disease. Nothing here diagnoses anything. This is a screen that decides whether the client goes on road today and whether they need an eye examination — the optometrist or ophthalmologist makes the finding, and the driver licensing authority decides the licence.

Ask, as well as test: night driving, glare, dusk, whether they have bumped anything on one side, when they last had their eyes examined, and whether the glasses they arrived in are the ones they drive in.

▸ Visual acuity — how to test it, and what 6/12 means
Acuity set-up · the distance the chart is calibrated for
Set-up
  • Chart at exactly the distance it is calibrated for, measured with a tape. A 6 m chart read at 5 m turns a 6/12 into an apparent 6/9 — this is the single most common way an OT vision screen goes wrong.
  • If you use a 3 m chart or a mirrored room, record the notation printed on that chart. Do not convert distances in your head.
  • Even lighting on the chart, no reflected glare, client's back to the window. Client seated upright, feet flat, chin level, not leaning forward.
Procedure
  • 1. Test them in the state they drive in. If they wear glasses to drive, test uncorrected and corrected. If they left their glasses at home, record the corrected figures as not obtained — do not estimate them.
  • 2. Occlude with an occluder or a cupped palm, never splayed fingers.
  • 3. Right eye, then left eye, then both eyes together. Testing binocularly only will hide a monocular loss; testing monocularly only will not give you the figure the private standard is written against.
  • 4. Read down until they make more than one error on a line. Record the smallest line read with no more than one letter missed, in the chart's own notation (6/12, 6/9−2).
  • 5. Encourage guessing — "have a go at the next line anyway". A cautious client who will not guess will test a line or two worse than they see.
  • 6. Record the figures, not a verdict. "6/9 binocular corrected" is a result; "vision normal" is not.
Austroads private vehicle standard. For an unconditional licence, binocular visual acuity of at least 6/12 — measured with corrective lenses if they are worn for driving. Commercial vehicle standards are higher and are set out in the vision chapter linked below; check them rather than working from memory. Where the standard is not met, the referral is to an optometrist or ophthalmologist and the decision is the driver licensing authority's, not yours.
Common errors
  • Pacing the distance instead of measuring it.
  • Testing with reading glasses rather than distance correction.
  • Letting the client lean forward, tilt the head, or peek between fingers.
  • Recording "within normal limits" with no figure — there is nothing for the licensing authority or the next assessor to compare against.
Further reading: Snellen chart — StatPearls · Austroads Assessing Fitness to Drive
▸ Visual fields by confrontation — technique, recording, and its limits
Confrontation · seating, and the four quadrants
Procedure
  • 1. Sit facing the client about a metre apart with your eyes at their eye level, plain background behind you, room well lit.
  • 2. Each eye separately first. Client covers their left eye; you cover your right, mirroring them. Both fixate on the other's open eye — you are comparing their field against your own.
  • 3. Bring a target — a wiggling finger, or a small white or red pin — in from beyond the periphery, halfway between the two of you, into each of the four quadrants in turn. The client says "now" the moment it appears.
  • 4. Then finger counting: present one, two or five fingers statically in each quadrant. A static target picks up defects a moving one does not.
  • 5. Then both eyes open. This is the binocular field the driving standard is written against, and the one that answers "will they see the pedestrian stepping off the kerb".
  • 6. Present a target on both sides simultaneously. A client who sees each side alone but only one when both appear has visual extinction — inattention rather than a field cut. It is a different finding with a different meaning on road, and it is what the Bells Test later in this section is looking for.
  • 7. Watch fixation the whole time. A client who searches with their eyes will pass a field test they should fail — and that searching is itself worth recording.
Record
  • Which quadrant or quadrants, and in which eye or both.
  • Whether the defect respects the vertical midline (points to a lesion behind the chiasm — a homonymous defect) or the horizontal (more often ocular).
  • Whether it was the same shape in each eye (congruous), and whether extinction was present on double simultaneous stimulation.
Austroads private vehicle standard. For an unconditional licence the binocular visual field must have a horizontal extent of at least 110°, within 10° above and below the horizontal midline, without significant field loss close to fixation. Formal perimetry — not confrontation — is what establishes this. Commercial standards are stricter; check the current chapter.
What confrontation will not do
  • It reliably picks up large defects — hemianopia, quadrantanopia, dense altitudinal loss. It misses arcuate scotoma in glaucoma, gradual constriction, and small paracentral loss.
  • It does not measure degrees, so it cannot tell you whether the 110° standard is met. A normal confrontation clears nobody.
  • Refer for formal perimetry (Esterman binocular field or equivalent) where there is stroke, brain injury, tumour, MS, glaucoma, retinitis pigmentosa, significant diabetic retinopathy, any previously documented defect — or where the client reports clipping kerbs, wing mirrors or door frames on one side.
What field loss looks like on road
  • Left homonymous — gap selection turning across oncoming traffic, and detection of vehicles approaching from the left at intersections.
  • Right homonymous — kerb-side pedestrians, signs, and merging to the left.
  • Inferior — kerbs, close obstacles, lane markings. Superior — traffic signals and overhead signage.
  • Expect to see the client compensating with head movement. Whether that compensation is consistent and automatic, or effortful and intermittent, is the on-road question — and it is what you will be watching for in Stage 3.
Further reading: Confrontation visual field testing · AFTD — vision and eye disorders
▸ Eye movements — pursuits, saccades, convergence and diplopia
The H pattern · six cardinal positions of gaze
Procedure
  • 1. Target about 40 cm from the client's face, their head still — steady their chin lightly if they keep turning it. Whether they move the head instead of the eyes is itself a finding, not a nuisance.
  • 2. Smooth pursuit. Move the target slowly through an H, pausing two or three seconds at each of the six end points. You are looking for smooth tracking versus jerky catch-up saccades ("cog-wheeling"), inability to hold end gaze, and nystagmus — note which gaze position provokes it.
  • 3. Saccades. Hold two targets roughly 30 cm apart and ask the client to look from one to the other on command, several times. Look for slowness to initiate, overshoot or undershoot needing a correction, and blinking or head movement used to launch the movement.
  • 4. Convergence. Bring the target slowly toward the bridge of the nose. Record the near point in centimetres at which they report doubling or one eye drifts out.
  • 5. Diplopia — ask directly, in each direction of gaze and at distance as well as near. Double vision in any position of gaze used for driving is a fitness-to-drive matter, not a comment in the notes.
  • 6. Ask whether any of it made them dizzy or nauseated. The sustained head-and-eye turn of a blind-spot check is the movement that provokes it, and you would rather find that out here than on a merge.
Why this matters more than it looks

Driving is a saccade task. A mirror check, a blind-spot check and a scan across an intersection are all sequences of large eye movements with a head turn on top, executed under time pressure while the vehicle keeps moving. Slow, inaccurate or effortful saccades produce exactly the on-road picture you will be rating in Stage 3 — late detection, "looked but did not see", head turns that are not real checks. Recording that link here is what lets the summary say the two halves of the assessment converged.

Record
  • Which movement, which direction, and what you saw — not "eye movements WNL".
  • Nystagmus: present or not, and in which gaze position. Near point of convergence in cm. Diplopia: yes or no, and where in the visual field.
  • Refer on for new diplopia, nystagmus that is not already known and explained, markedly saccadic pursuit, or any defect found on confrontation.
Further reading: Neurologic examination — StatPearls (cranial nerves III, IV and VI)

Manual muscle testing (MRC grades)
The screens above establish what the client can do; this grid measures the power behind it. Nothing here is pre-filled as normal. Each muscle group starts as not yet tested and you record the grade you actually elicited — the report prints only what you tested, and says plainly what you did not. Every group carries a how to test this one card: position, what to stabilise, the words to say, where the resistance goes, and the error that most often produces a wrong grade.
▸ Muscle testing reference — MRC grading & technique
MRC / Oxford scale
  • 0 — No contraction palpable.
  • 1 — Flicker or trace of contraction, no joint movement.
  • 2 — Active movement through full range with gravity eliminated.
  • 3 — Active movement through full range against gravity, but not against resistance.
  • 4 — Active movement through full range against gravity and some resistance (4−/4/4+ for mild, moderate, strong resistance).
  • 5 — Normal power: full range against gravity and full resistance.
Technique
  • Position and stabilise the joint proximally so the target muscle is isolated; demonstrate the movement first.
  • Take the limb to end of available range, then apply resistance at the end of range, building gradually — a "break test" — rather than pushing against a moving limb.
  • Test the unaffected side first to establish that client's normal, then compare sides.
  • Grade 4 vs 5 is the common error: if you can break the hold at all, it is a 4, not a 5. Record 4−/4/4+ rather than defaulting to 5.
  • Note pain, effort, fatigue or inconsistency separately from the grade — a grade limited by pain is recorded as such, not as weakness.
  • Do not record a grade for a group you did not physically test. Leave it as not tested and say why.
Why these groups for driving
  • Shoulder/elbow/wrist and grip — steering control, rapid corrective steering, handbrake, gear selection, seatbelt.
  • Hip flexion, knee extension/flexion, ankle dorsiflexion/plantarflexion — pedal transfer speed and sustained braking force.
  • Cervical and trunk rotation — shoulder checks, blind-spot checks, reversing.
